Camping Chairs: Comfort at the Campsite

After a day on the trail, the difference between a good evening and a sore one is often a decent chair. Sitting on a log or a rock works for ten minutes; a real camping chair lets you cook, eat, read, and watch the fire without your back complaining. The trick is picking a chair that fits your trip — a car-camping lounge chair and a backpacking stool are very different tools.

Car camping: go for comfort

When the car carries the weight, comfort wins. Look for a chair with a high back, padded armrests, and a seat wide enough to sit in for an hour without shifting around. A built-in cup holder is genuinely useful at camp — it keeps your coffee off the dirt. Oversized and reclining chairs are popular for a reason: after a long hike, sinking into a chair that actually supports your lower back is one of the small pleasures of camping. The only real cost is space in the car, and that is a fair trade on a family trip.

Backpacking: grams matter

On the trail, every gram counts, so a backpacking chair is a compromise — a low stool, a folding tripod seat, or a chair kit that turns your sleeping pad into a backrest. These are not as comfortable as a car-camping chair, but they get you off the cold ground and save your knees at dinner. If you are counting weight, ask whether the chair earns its place: on a short overnight, a sit pad may be enough; on a multi-day trip, a light chair is worth it for most people.

What to check before you buy

Check the weight rating first — chairs are rated for a maximum load, and a chair rated below your weight will sag or fail. Sit in it before you buy if you can: the seat height should let your feet rest flat, and the armrests should be where your arms actually fall. Look at the fabric and frame — steel frames are strong but heavy, aluminium is lighter and rust-resistant, and ripstop fabric holds up better than thin nylon. A carry bag with a shoulder strap makes the chair much easier to haul from the car to the site.

Look after it

Shake off dirt and dry the chair before packing it away; damp fabric left in a bag will smell by the next trip. Keep the chair out of the fire's heat — synthetic fabric melts faster than you expect. A chair that is cleaned and dried after each trip lasts for years.

Pick the chair for the trip you actually take

One chair rarely does everything. Many campers keep a comfortable chair for car camping and a light one for the trail. Before you buy, check the route and the site on the CampAndTrailGuide trail map so you know whether you are walking in or driving up. The camping gear guide covers chairs alongside tents, stoves, and sleeping gear, and the CampAndTrailGuide homepage has state guides and trip-planning help for the whole trip.

A good chair is cheap comfort. Buy the one that fits your trip, sit in it before you commit, and your evenings at camp will be a lot better for it.
